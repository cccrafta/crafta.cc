## Summary
Peer-reviewed article (Sport in History 35(1), pp. 19-45, Manchester Metropolitan University authors) on how middle-class amateur clubs used Classical precedent, science and clothing to separate "university athletes" from working-class professionals. Publisher page returned 403; claims below come from the abstract as reported by search results and from Jean Williams's summary of the article in the issue introduction. Full text not read, so stub.

## Key Claims
- By 1837 England had a number of professional pedestrians competing in events covered by the sporting press; they distinguished themselves with "colours" and distinctive athletic clothing (abstract).
- From the eighteenth century, professional pedestrians, boxers, jockeys, rowers and others wore colours to represent their (often aristocratic, gambling) backer (Williams summary, p. 8).
- Later in the century a public-school and university-educated middle class formed clubs and associations that challenged professional dominance; the athlete's somatotype and clothing strategies changed as a result (abstract).
- The amateur ethos assumed innate physical superiority and preferred the elegant all-rounder to the muscular, specialised bodies of working-class professionals, drawing heavily on the Classical world (abstract).
- Authors describe a "university costume" that reinforced the visual distinction between amateur and professional bodies (abstract).
- Per Williams: the "ceremony of peeling" let spectators assess fitness before wagering; a working-class preference for the specialist gave way to a middle-class taste for those "who won without seeming to work very hard", which "gradually became a prevailing amateur ethos in dress, status and taste" (Williams, p. 9).
- Rules by amateur associations often stated what a participant may and may not wear (Williams, p. 9).
