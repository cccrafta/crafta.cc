## Summary
Albert Muzquiz's history (published Aug 2018, updated Aug 2024) of the rugby shirt as a garment engineered for a full-contact game that migrated into Ivy, preppy and climbing wardrobes. Distinguishes it from the polo and lists current makers.

## Key Claims
- Rugby began at Rugby School, England, in the 1830s; organised clubs spread across the UK over the next ~20 years.
- Early uniforms were matching wool jumpers with white trousers; wool was itchy and heavy, so players moved to heavy-gauge cotton shirts, which became the modern rugby shirt.
- The classic shirt is long-sleeved, striped or colourful, with a contrast collar; the article argues it has changed less than the polo, whose original button-down collar (kept from riding) Brooks Brothers copied for its oxford shirt.
- Rugby revival in the 1960s-70s made the shirt a staple of Ivy League and preppy style; Ralph Lauren has made fashion rugby shirts since.
- Late-1960s California rock climbers wore them because the tough construction resisted abrasion on Yosemite rock; Patagonia (founded 1973 by climber Yvon Chouinard) went on to make rugby shirts.
- Modern playing shirts are synthetic, breathable, tear-resistant and cut tight so opponents cannot grab them, with rubber grip bubbles; they no longer resemble the classic collared stripe, so the heritage version is now a separate garment from the sport one.
- Makers recommended: Heimat (Germany, made in Portugal), Lost & Found (Canada, 12 oz pre-shrunk cotton), Barbarian Sportswear (Canada, rugby jerseys since 1981, knits its own fabric).
