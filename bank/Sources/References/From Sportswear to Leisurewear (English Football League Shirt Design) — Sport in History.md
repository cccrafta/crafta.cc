## Summary
Peer-reviewed study (Sport in History 35(1), pp. 156-194; DOI 10.1080/17460263.2014.986518; open-access author version on White Rose) of how the club football shirt moved from team uniform to children's replica to adult leisurewear and finally a "heritage" product. Gives concrete dates, firms and deal terms for the uniform -> leisure -> nostalgia sequence. Read from the open-access PDF (abstract, introduction, sections on the child replica and the 1970s).

## Key Claims
- Football-kit supply to English professional clubs was dominated by Bukta (est. 1879; first elite client Nottingham Forest, 1884) and Umbro (est. 1924 by Harold and Wallace Humphrey) before the 1970s.
- Before copyright, no firm could stop rivals making the same club's kit; at the start of 1973/74 both Umbro and Admiral (Leicester) sold child-size Manchester United kits.
- Late-1960s kit design was minimalist: by the end of 1968/69 over three-quarters of English Football League clubs played in plain shirts, a historical low for patterning.
- Admiral signed the first exclusive copyrighted-kit licence with Leeds United in 1973 under the 1968 Copyright Act: an initial fee of £10,000 against a 5% royalty on sales of the club's Admiral clothing and leisurewear (recalled by Admiral owner Bert Patrick). Most clubs signed similar deals within about five years.
- Shirt sponsorship was pioneered by non-league Kettering Town in 1976.
- The replica industry "began by producing replica sportswear for children, but within two decades had transformed the football shirt into an adult leisurewear garment".
- Once the replica was adult leisurewear, "retrospective and recycled shirt designs" presented a club's identity through heritage and appealed to fans' nostalgic ideas of authenticity, spawning a parallel retro-replica industry.
- Authors argue this nostalgia caused stagnation in the shirt's aesthetic development, and that the contemporary shirt is now cast as a "heritage" product.
