## Summary
Peer-reviewed article (Sport in History 35(1), pp. 127-155) on how a basketball shoe built for performance acquired meanings its maker never intended in New York hip hop. Only the abstract was available through search; the full text (Academia.edu copy) was not read, so stub. Useful for the "meaning is not fixed by design" claim.

## Key Claims
- By the 1960s adidas, based in Bavaria, was the world's leading sports footwear maker, dominating elite sport through promotion and innovative shoes (abstract).
- The Superstar was designed to take the basketball market from American rubber companies; over the next two decades it took on meanings adidas never intended, becoming linked with hip hop, a youth subculture born in 1970s New York (abstract).
- Argument: design is shaped by use, and consumption is allied to practice; young New York consumers shaped how the shoe was understood, and adidas later commodified those readings (abstract).
- Conclusion: "a product's meaning is never fixed"; producers and consumers are in constant dialogue over use and perception (abstract, paraphrased).
- Jean Williams's introduction separately dates the Adidas training shoe's 1954 launch as the point sportswear became "a style trend worn on the street" (see Kit introduction note).
