## Summary
Peer-reviewed introduction by Jean Williams (De Montfort University) to the first special issue of *Sport in History* on dress and sport (vol. 35, no. 1, March 2015, pp. 1-18; reissued as the Routledge book *Kit: Fashioning the Sporting Body*, 2016). It surveys sport and dress history from the Georgian era to replica kits and explicitly names the workwear/sportswear overlap as an under-researched gap. Text read from the publisher's open preview of the Routledge reissue; page numbers below are the journal pagination.

## Key Claims
- Sport and costume history both concern "bodily practice and embodied experience"; sport "crosses boundaries between everyday dress, ritualized performance and the sartorial avant garde" (pp. 2).
- "Many people would have adapted their everyday dress for competition and therefore the overlap between workwear and sportswear remains a huge area for further research"; military and other uniforms complicate adaptations from the basic Norfolk jacket (p. 8).
- Georgian sports clothing was defined by class-based systems of dress: field and water sports "for the classes", pugilism, swimming and pedestrianism "for the masses" (p. 3).
- Quoting Elizabeth Wilson: the landowning gentry were "rural capitalists" and "it was their daily, working dress that became the nineteenth century uniform" (pp. 3-4). Sports clothing diversified from the mid-19th century, often from manufacturers solving British weather.
- Uniforms were "the first mass-produced items" (police, railway employees, nurses, servants); "working overalls in factories" emphasised the collective nature of working life "and this passed into sport and leisure" (p. 4).
- From the 1860s the Norfolk jacket moved from its tweed field-sport form into town wear and, in a boys' version with knickerbockers, into standard street-wear (p. 4).
- Trade-mark and brand dates cited: Aquascutum (John Emery, shower-proof textile trade-marked 1853); Thomas Burberry founded 1856, gabardine 1880, logo trademark 1900; Slazenger trading from 1881; Corah's St Margaret knitwear and jersey trademarked 1883, making yachting, rugby and football jerseys (pp. 5, 7).
- Redfern (Cowes) with patronage of Alexandra, Princess of Wales, sold the "Redfern Suit" for active women "of all classes from actresses to royalty"; shops in New York, Rhode Island, Edinburgh and Paris by the 1890s (p. 5).
- "In the 1920s couturier Coco Chanel made her name by adapting workers' flannel, often worn as underwear, as outerwear for the elite" (p. 7). Stated without a source in the text; treat as [single-source].
- "As fewer of us made our own clothes... 'sportswear' came to mean not just what was worn but an overall look", usually "separates"; the "smart casual" dress code "is based on the idea of sportswear as the chosen type of dressing for most everyday functions, be they work or leisure" (pp. 8, 10).
- Williams notes American sportswear has "the most sustained academic literature" while British sports clothing is under-researched (p. 10); by 1954 the Adidas training shoe made sportswear "a style trend worn on the street, rather than on the pitch" (p. 11).
- Summarising Day and Oldfield: amateur principles led by a public-school and university-educated middle-class elite curtailed professional dominance through codification and clubs; rules often stated what a participant might wear (pp. 8-9).
