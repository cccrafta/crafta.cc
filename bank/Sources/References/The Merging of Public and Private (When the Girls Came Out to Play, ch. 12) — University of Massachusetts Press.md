## Summary
Closing chapter (pp. 242-247) of Warner's *When the Girls Came Out to Play: The Birth of American Sportswear* (University of Massachusetts Press, 2006), open access on UMass ScholarWorks. Warner argues modern casual sportswear originated in American women's college gym and crew clothing, not Paris, and became "American Style" when public and private sport clothes merged by the 1930s. Reviewed by Gerald Gems (Journal of American History 93:4, 2007) and Annie Gilbert Coleman (American Historical Review 112:3, 2007); both reviews read only as abstracts.

## Key Claims
- The middy-bloomer gym outfit became "the uniform of schoolgirls everywhere by the late teens and 1920s" because "it was tough, hard-wearing, easy and comfortable, even if somewhat bulky", and signified "a casual but reasonable rejection of the social proprieties" (p. 242).
- These exercise outfits "predated by over a decade the lean, pared-down fashion wear Chanel and Patou claimed to have introduced for sport"; inspiration came from American women's higher education, "far removed from Paris fashion houses" (p. 242).
- Team sports and the press drove acceptance: Princeton v Rutgers football 1867, Harvard v Yale from 1875; "Sports, then, almost unwittingly, accomplished what no amount of dress reform had been able to achieve" (p. 243).
- Knitting and sporting-goods firms: Munsingwear (Minneapolis, 1886, knit underwear; claimed the classic collared cotton knit golf shirt); Wright & Ditson (Boston, 1871; bought out by the Spalding brothers in 1891, name continuing into the 1940s); Jantzen (Portland, 1910, began as Portland Knitting Company making heavy wool sweaters, socks and gloves; 1913 striped wool rib one-piece swimsuit for the Portland Rowing Club, patented 1921, eight pounds when wet) (pp. 243-244).
- "It was mass production as much as anything else that led to an overall simplification in dress": less hand-cutting and fitting meant "straight-cut, and loose" garments for the masses (p. 244).
- Chanel's tricots drew on the sweaters and knit shirts of her lover Boy Capel, "based strictly on sport apparel"; Jean Patou made the tennis dress Suzanne Lenglen first wore in 1919 (p. 245).
- Hollywood amplified casual California dress; "the category of sportswear was born" with the fitted, separates-based outfits of the 1930s (pp. 246-247).
- Trousers on women: knickerbockers and beach pajamas opened the door in the 1920s but were not worn in public; Katharine Hepburn's trousers predated "the defense-worker pants of World War II"; widespread acceptance took until the 1980s (p. 247).
- Thesis line: "Now the entire world wears American sportswear" (p. 247). Publisher abstract: this is "the first time in the history of the world that such universality has existed in clothing".
- Reviewers: Gems calls it the first scholarly study systematically tracing US women's sportswear, about twenty years in the making, and reports Warner calling it the most significant clothing of the twentieth century, "undeniably American" yet worn worldwide (p. 3 per Gems). Coleman reports that students believed the clothing "suited work as well as play", and that by 1910 women wore middies, bathing dresses and gym suits on Woods Hole collecting trips.
