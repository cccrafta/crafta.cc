## Summary
Museum curatorial account of how rugby kit moved from Rugby School dress to 1871 internationals to 2003 synthetic jerseys and professional sponsorship. Strong on collars, fabric weight and commercial shift; silent on specific 19th-century weaves.

## Key Claims
- Rugby School sportswear of the mid-1800s was the template for the kit of the first international sides in 1871.
- "The dominant jersey fabric was cotton and occasionally wool"; white was the usual sports colour at Rugby School, easy to obtain and boil-clean; white cotton duck trousers arrived in the 1830s alongside rising Lancashire output.
- "It has been estimated that the cotton jersey retained up to 3kg of water when worn in wet conditions."
- Rugby School's 1846 resolution: one side in white, the other in stripes, replacing caps as the way to tell teams apart.
- Collars: England jerseys had button-up collars until 2003; around 1900 other sides wore roll necks or lace-up collars; modern jerseys have none because collars give tacklers something to grab; "loose jerseys were deemed to aid the opposition in the tackle".
- Rugby League V-necks introduced soon after the Northern Union formed in 1895.
- Numbering: first used 1897 (New Zealand v Queensland); 1967 IRB required numbering with the full-back as 15.
- 2003: Nike Dri-Fit jerseys for England, France and South Africa, 62% cotton, 34% polyester, 4% spandex, 154g lighter than cotton dry and 362g lighter wet, after a two-year consultation; mesh panels and rubber grip prints.
- Professionalism began in 1995; manufacturer marks became prominent, appearing on British and Irish Lions tracksuits from the early 1970s. Closing line frames the shift as "a schoolboy pastime into a global sporting-corporate showpiece".
