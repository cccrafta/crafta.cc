## Summary
Open-access peer-reviewed article (Contemporary British History, published online 9 Nov 2023) using hooligan memoirs to map British "casual" culture of the late 1970s to late 1980s. Useful for the sportswear-as-working-class-identity step after the decline of manual work. Read from the open PDF (introduction and background sections; abstract not extracted).

## Key Claims
- Jones argues the casual subculture, "influential not least upon the fashion designers, buyers and journalists of the 1990s", had received limited attention from sociologists and, "as far as I know, none from academic historians".
- Starting in Liverpool and Manchester in the late 1970s, "by the mid-1980s the 'casual' style of high-end European sportswear and designer clothing dominated the terraces".
- Aquascutum, Armani, Versace and Burberry "displaced the platform boots, flares and scarves of the seventies"; casuals wore expensive prêt-à-porter menswear at a time of mass unemployment.
- Memoir examples: Cardiff fans in Pringle and Burberry; a trip to Amsterdam for rare Adidas trainers; Trim Trab trainers and Pringle sweaters replacing press trousers.
- Academic orthodoxy (Dunning and colleagues at Leicester, after Norbert Elias) cast hooligans as a "rough" unincorporated working-class fraction; Jones argues this lagged behind the terraces' style and affluence.
- He argues football-supporting gained symbolic importance in underpinning working-class masculinities "as manual work cultures have declined due to deindustrialisation".
- Note for Crafta: the source frames casual dress as "high-end European sportswear and designer clothing", not worn-out training kit; it does not say the garments were functional.
