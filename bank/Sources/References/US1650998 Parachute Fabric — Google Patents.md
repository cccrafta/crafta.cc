## Summary
Patent for a silk parachute cloth woven from groups of threads so that a tear stays local instead of spreading. It is a documented precursor of ripstop structure, although the text never uses the word ripstop and does not name nylon. Full text was readable but OCR-garbled in places.
## Key Claims
- Inventor Hiram T. Nones of Washington, D.C.; no assignee; filed 4 September 1926, granted 29 November 1927; expired 1944.
- Problem: tightly folded parachute fabric creases sharply and the creases weaken fibres, so rips are likelier under the shock of opening; sliding folds also cause friction burns.
- Key claim: "Rips, perforations, bullet holes, in fabric of my invention are localized and do not spread or enlarge."
- Structure: warp and weft each made of groups of threads (for example a flat five-strand warp, or groups of two, three or four) interlacing as groups, with binder threads; small gaps form where groups cross.
- Reported tests against the best earlier fabric: tearing resistance 6.18 lb warp and 6.27 lb filling versus 1.81 and 4.01; ripping resistance 18 and 15 lb per inch versus 8 and 11; air permeability 125.4 versus 62.9 cubic feet per minute.
- Material is "generally made" of silk, including cheaper raw silk grades; cotton is not named; clothing is mentioned as another use.
- The patent never uses the word "ripstop", so it documents the principle (grouped reinforcing threads) rather than the later nylon grid product.
