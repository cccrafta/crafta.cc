## Summary
Official US Army history (Erna Risch, US Army in World War II "Green Book", CMH Pub 10-12), pp. 99-101, covering the Army's jungle-clothing trials from Panama in 1941-42 to the 1945 poplin uniform. It is the best-sourced account of why heavy HBT failed in the jungle and why the Quartermaster Corps moved to a thin, dense poplin cloth.

## Key Claims
- Cresson Kearny, Panama Mobile Force, tested a dyed HBT one-piece suit and jungle gear (boot, ration, hammock) in 1941-42 (pp. 99-101; Kearny detail is also in US WW2 Uniforms).
- MacArthur asked for 150,000 sets of jungle equipment in late July 1942; the Army Ground Forces approved a one-piece HBT jungle suit on 28 Jul 1942, with cargo pockets, tight cuffs and internal suspenders to take weight off the shoulders (better ventilation, fewer insect bites) (p. 100; corroborated by US WW2 Uniforms, so two independent sources).
- In New Guinea the HBT "frogskin" suit was reported "too heavy, too hot, and too uncomfortable" (QM observer, 1943 report, cited on p. 100). Wet HBT gained substantial weight, and the missing drop seat defeated the anti-malaria purpose; a two-piece suit replaced it in spring 1943. The cloth is HBT, not denim; any extension to denim is inference (same class of heavy cotton).
- Aug 1943: the Quartermaster Corps started a jungle-clothing project (Everglades field study, heat and humidity physiology tests at Indiana University, wear tests at Camp Lee). The Textile Section sought a cloth "thin, dense, and of the lowest possible water-holding capacity". Poplin and Byrd cloth were cooler, lighter dry, absorbed less water, dried faster and gave better mosquito protection as tight weaves; the Committee on Jungle Clothing recommended poplin (p. 100).
- Prototypes were tested Jul-Nov 1944 on Bougainville and Biak and in Burma. A 5-oz poplin jungle uniform was standardized on 11 Jul 1945 but never issued, because the war ended and poplin was short (p. 101). Reading it as the direct ancestor of the 1960s 5.5-oz poplin jacket is inference.
- Gas flaps (flies and gussets at sleeve and front openings) were imposed on all HBT clothing by the Chemical Warfare Service and were "disliked intensely by men serving in the jungles" (p. 99). The inner gas flap reappears on the 1963 jacket (see the VietnamGear and Rare Gear USA notes).
