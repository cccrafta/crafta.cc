## Summary
The patent behind what Champion later sold as Reverse Weave. It describes a sweatshirt cut so the knit ribs run horizontally, solving shrinkage in length and seam failure at the sleeves. It is the inventor's own technical statement of why the garment was built the way it was, for football players wearing shoulder pads.

## Key Claims
- Inventor Samuel N. Friedland, assignor to Champion Knitwear Company, Inc., Rochester, N.Y. Filed 20 January 1938 (Serial No. 185,984), granted 9 August 1938.
- Problem stated: in ordinary sweat shirts the ribs run vertically, so after washing the garment "necessarily becomes much shorter"; the ribbed waistband cannot be pulled back down, and re-stretching strains the shoulders and arms, causing "objectionable rips, tears splitting seams."
- Conventional shirts were made from at least four separate pieces with inset sleeves; the sleeve seams were the weak points.
- Method: start with a tube of knitted fabric "in knitted courses run lengthwise thereof", cut across it in alternately reversed garment blanks that interlock, so there is "very little wastage material". Body and sleeves form one piece.
- Result: "wales thereof disposed substantially horizontally throughout body portion", including across the shoulders, with enlarged armpit portions cut at an obtuse angle.
- Benefit: the body can be pulled lengthwise after laundering to undo shrinkage, and it tends to reduce in circumference "thereby conforming more snugly" to the body.
- The patent says the stretch around shoulders suits wearers using shoulder pads, as in football.
- Durability: no seam where sleeve meets body; the only seams run along the sides and "along underside sleeves where subject less strain".
- Note: the 1938 date is a grant (filed January 1938). Champion's own site gives "invented 1934, applied 1938, granted 1952", conflating this with the 1952 patent (US 2,613,360).
