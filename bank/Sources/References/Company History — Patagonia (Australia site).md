## Summary
Patagonia's own account of Chouinard Equipment turning from climbing hardware to clothing, including the 1970 rugby shirt, the 1972 clothing line-up, and the Malden Mills and Milliken fabric stories. A maker T1 source; the main patagonia.com history page was down when tried, so this regional mirror was used. WebFetch summary, quotes limited.
## Key Claims
- Chouinard went on a winter climbing trip to Scotland in 1970 and 'bought a regulation-team rugby shirt to wear rock climbing'.
- The page says the shirt was overbuilt for rugby and its collar kept gear slings off his neck. It describes it as blue with one yellow and two red stripes across the chest. (Heddels says yellow and blue striped; see dispute note in the Heddels Patagonia note.)
- By 1972 the company sold 'rugby shirts from England, polyurethane rain cagoules and bivouac sacks from Scotland', plus boiled-wool gloves and mittens from Austria and hand-knit beanies from Boulder.
- The company 'began to see clothing as a way to help support the marginally profitable hardware business'.
- By 1970 Chouinard Equipment was the largest US supplier of climbing hardware; its pitons damaged rock, which led to scaling back and to aluminum chocks in the 1972 catalog.
- Capilene: in 1984 Chouinard saw Milliken's polyester football jerseys at a Chicago sporting goods show; Milliken's etched-fibre process moved moisture away from the body. 'Chouinard saw the fabric as perfect for underwear, and Capilene polyester was born.' This is a sportswear-to-technical-outdoor route.
- Synchilla (fall 1985) was developed with Malden Mills as a softer, non-pilling double-faced fleece; polypropylene and bunting products had been 70 percent of sales before the move to Capilene.
- The page does not mention Umbro, Baggies, or the 'Patagonia' brand name date; those must be sourced elsewhere.
