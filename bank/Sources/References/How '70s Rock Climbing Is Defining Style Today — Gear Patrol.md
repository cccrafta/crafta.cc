## Summary
Charles McFarlane's Gear Patrol piece (9 May 2019, updated 25 Aug 2022) on 1970s US climbing kit, quoting historian Rachel Gross and Patagonia archive staffer Val Franco. Best accessible source linking army surplus, painter pants and rugby shirts to the rigours of climbing. T3 media but with named expert quotes. WebFetch summary.
## Key Claims
- Dr. Rachel Gross (University of Montana postdoc, historian of American outdoor clothing) is quoted: 'The use of a lot of military goods, workwear and even rugby shirts in climbing speak to the particular rigors you encounter climbing.'
- Gross: most Americans in outdoor sports had to get clothing 'from department stores or from army surplus stores'; specialist brands were scarce and climbers 'had to make their own kits'.
- Best-value pants were old army fatigues or white painter's pants, 'dirt cheap, hard wearing and loose fitting'; OG-107 patch-pocket fatigues had ample pockets and voluminous legs; painter pants were 'the most ubiquitous choice of the era'.
- Gross also says there is 'very little opportunity to adjust clothing' while climbing, so a garment must work across temperature and wind without changes.
- The rugby shirt was 'the most iconic garment of a '70s climbing kit'; how it reached Western climbers is 'slightly contested', with the Chouinard Scotland 1970 story the leading account.
- Val Franco (Patagonia archive): 'there was a lot of metal that you were wearing around your neck' and the collar 'allowed you to rest that on your neck and not get chafed'. Chouinard first imported shirts from England, then made them; Franco ran Patagonia's first sewing shop in 1973 and recalls 12 original colourways.
- The only Dickies reference is a modern product pick (Dickies painter's pants, $22): no 1970s Dickies, Carhartt or Levi's claim is made.
- Recommended visual sources: the 2014 documentary Valley Uprising and Glen Denny's Yosemite in the Sixties.
