## Summary
Interview with historian Rachel S. Gross (University of Colorado Denver; Yale University Press, 2024) on how the US outdoor industry grew out of military surplus and WWII Quartermaster Corps work. Academic expertise via retailer blog. She does not discuss workwear as a category or Chouinard. WebFetch summary.
## Key Claims
- After WWI the US Army saw that heavy wool greatcoats suited standing still but not carrying loads; early in WWII the Quartermaster Corps called in outdoor-industry figures (Eddie Bauer, L.L.Bean, Harold Hirsch of Hirsch-Weis) as civilian consultants.
- The M-1943 system replaced the overcoat with several thin layers; Gross calls it 'so imperfect' because many soldiers never got the full set, and the Corps ran classes teaching layering.
- Expert mountaineers tied to these industry figures consulted for or enlisted in the Quartermaster Corps.
- The Khaki Gang (late 1940s to early 1950s hikers and climbers) wore military surplus and built an 'anti-fashion' around olive and khaki; Gross says this predates surplus as an anti-war symbol.
- Surplus designs often fell short, so military gear became 'a starting point for people to design against'.
- 1970s boom: Gross says demand came from enthusiasm for the woods, not new technology; Dale Johnson's Frostline sewing kits were '30 to 40% cheaper' than ready-made clothing and at least 12 other companies sold DIY kits at the peak.
- She criticises 'heritage' marketing that centres 'white male laborers, working-class authenticity' and military men as a narrow history.
- Not covered: workwear as a category, Chouinard, Yosemite.
