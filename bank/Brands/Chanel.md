## In One Line
Paris couture house that borrowed from sport, sailors and workwear; in the vault it appears as an adopter of other makers' garments, not as a maker of its own lineage.

## Lineage
Chanel's Deauville boutique opened in 1913 and stripes appear in the 1917 collection [^4]. A chapter on American women's sport argues that college gym outfits predated Chanel's and Patou's sport fashion by a decade, and that Chanel's tricots drew on the sport sweaters of Boy Capel [^1]. The Sport in History introduction states "Chanel adapted workers' flannel"; this is a single-source claim, and the vault's research contradicts it [^2]. The STITCH essay says the little black dress took inspiration from maids' uniforms, a claim that is a blog assertion [^3].

## Making and Material
Chanel acquired Barrie Knitwear in 2012 through its Métiers d'Art initiative, buying a Scottish cashmere maker [^5]. No source in the vault describes how Chanel makes its own garments.

## Today
Sells at luxury prices; the vault holds no price data or trusted review [unsourced]. The vault's London research files the house under cachet or no lineage evidence.

## Design
The house's own contributions, jersey tailoring and the stripe in a couture frame, are well documented in general fashion history [unsourced]. Taste score 4 reflects the cultural reach of the silhouette, not the construction.

## Depth
Several sources touch Chanel and none treats it as a subject. It is most useful as a counter-case: a house borrowing from sailors, sportswear and workers without the maker lineage.
