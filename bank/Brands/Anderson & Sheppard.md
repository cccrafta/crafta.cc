## In One Line
A bespoke tailor near Savile Row, established 1906, known for the soft drape cut. It is the house where cut and construction are best documented in the vault.

## Lineage
Established 1906; the bespoke business is at 32 Old Burlington Street, London, and holds a royal warrant as tailors to the King [^1]. The drape style originated with Frederick Scholte (soft shoulder, high armhole, large sleevehead) and became the house style [^2]. Fred Astaire was a client [^3].

## Making and Material
The body canvas is wool or horsehair, loosely woven and cut on the bias at about 30 degrees so it can stretch; most tailors cut it at right angles for rigidity [^2]. Chest stitches run an inch or more, longer than peers, and the shoulder seam is sewn by hand for movement, where some very expensive tailors machine-sew it [^2]. The maker's page links to its workrooms, Process and Team pages but does not say who makes the garments, so in-house making is unconfirmed [^1]. An off-cut house tweed is promoted as a circular initiative [^1]. Dashing Tweeds delivered cloth to the house from 2006 [^6].

## Patina and Care
No source on repair or recutting of the house's garments. Astaire threw new suits against walls to remove stiffness, which says something about how the cut is meant to wear in [^3].

## Today
Permanent Style places the house's drape cut among the cheaper Row houses [^5]. The house makes a safari jacket with 15 pockets [^4]. It displays B Corp and Planet Mark badges [^1].

## Design
Soft shoulders, a full chest and back and a closely cut waist [^5]. Astaire's fittings show the logic: a high armhole skimming the armpit and a collar cut close for stability while dancing [^3]. The look is recognisable and distinct from Huntsman's structure.

## Depth
A named cut, a documented canvas and a documented client. The Astaire fitting process, the safari jacket and the Dashing Tweeds cloth link are all research threads already open in the vault.
