## In One Line
A protected Scottish tweed cloth that British makers use for overcoats and jackets, mentioned in the vault only through the garments that use it.

## Lineage
No source in the vault describes the cloth's origin, the Orb mark or the Harris Tweed Act. [unsourced] The cloth is handwoven in the Outer Hebrides, and the Orb mark is protected by an Act of Parliament. The vault's evidence is the downstream use: Margaret Howell cites Harris Tweed as a British tradition she works with [^5].

## Making and Material
The vault has no source on spinning, weaving or finishing. [unsourced] Handweaving in island homes is the cloth's defining condition. The Universal Works story note, a low-reliability search summary, says the Kenneth Mackenzie mill supplies the cloth [^2]. Gloverall lists Harris Tweed among its fabrics (Italian wool blends, melton, Harris Tweed) and for overcoats [^3][^4].

## Patina and Care
No source in the vault. [unsourced] Tweed softens and takes shape with wear.

## Today
Nigel Cabourn rebuilt a 1943 flight suit in Harris Tweed and uses it and Ventile often [^1]. Gloverall still makes the Original Monty to its 1951 pattern [^4]. Margaret Howell works with it as part of a British cloth tradition [^5].

## Design
A dense, textured wool cloth that holds a coat's shape and shows its colours flecked rather than flat. The vault's evidence is of makers choosing it, not of the cloth's hand.

## Depth
A research note on the Orb mark, the Act, and the weaving model would fill a gap, plus how makers such as Cabourn and Howell use the cloth in garments [^1][^5].
