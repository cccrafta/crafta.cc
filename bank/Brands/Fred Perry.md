## In One Line
A tennis-born polo brand whose twin-tipped shirt became a uniform for mods, skinheads, rude boys and ravers, which makes it the vault's case of a sport garment turning into subculture dress.

## Lineage
The brand's own page says the first shirt was worn in 1952, all white with a laurel wreath on the left, and that the twin tipping on the collar came about ten years later [^1]. That is single-source maker material; the page does not name Tibby Wegner, the M3 model or the fabric. Search extracts credit Wegner and a Leicester-made sweatband, but colour details disagree, so those stay unverified. The Oxford Student describes Fred Perry and Lacoste as founded by sportsmen [^3].

## Making and Material
The vault has no source on where or how current shirts are made, or on the cloth. The one cloth fact is a collaboration: Dashing Tweeds wove a reflective 3M yarn with British and Shetland wool for Fred Perry, an idea that came from scraped-up double-yellow-line paint [^5].

## Patina and Care
No source in the vault.

## Today
The brand's page says the shirt was adopted by mods, skinheads, rude boys and ravers, "a uniform that unites the working class and music scenes" [^1]. The vault's contemporary Harrington review notes a slimmer, streamlined silhouette with laurel-wreath branding [^2]. Collaborators include Raeburn and Nigel Cabourn [^6]. Skinheads wore Harringtons with Fred Perry polos and Ben Sherman shirts [^4].

## Design
A plain knit polo with a ribbed, two-stripe collar and a small laurel; the tipping is the signature. The piece works because the collar stripes make a sport shirt legible as a uniform.

## Depth
The subculture lineage (mod to skinhead to ravers) and the Dashing Tweeds work are documented. The official site was not read; only third-party blogs and the product page were. The Harrington note carries the strongest research thread.
