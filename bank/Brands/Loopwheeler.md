## In One Line
A Japanese sweatshirt maker founded to keep Wakayama's hanging loopwheel knitting machines running. Its fabric is the product, and it is the vault's best case of a slow machine defining quality.

## Lineage
Satoshi Suzuki founded Loopwheeler in 1999 to make "the most authentic sweatshirts" and to stop the tsuriamiki machines and their fabric disappearing.[^1] Loop wheel machines were imported from Europe in the early 20th century; from the 1960s faster, later computerised machines replaced them, and a few in Wakayama kept running and evolved into the Japanese tsuriamiki, hung from the ceiling so yarn drops by its own weight.[^1] The company is therefore a preservation business, not a continuous 1920s maker, which is why Lineage is 4.

## Making and Material
Two Wakayama factories run the machines: Kanekichi Industry (established 1920) and Wada Meriyasu; sewing is at Maruwa Textile's Aomori factory, flat-seam on a four-needle machine, all in Japan from yarn to garment.[^1] Each size is knit on a machine of matching diameter, so garments have no side seams.[^1] The maker puts output at about 1 m of sweatshirt fabric per hour (pique about 0.3 m) against over 10 m per hour on a sinker machine; these are its own figures with no second source.[^1] Heddels describes loopwheeled fabric as plump with a slightly irregular, slubby texture and says loopwheeled items cost roughly twice as much as others.[^2]

## Patina and Care
The maker claims sinker-knit fabric stiffens with washing and loses texture after about three years, while vintage sweatshirts of the late 1940s to mid-1960s remain prized.[^1] A retailer says loopwheel fabric "gradually conforms to the body" and develops a patina hard to replicate; this is a seller's assertion with no testing, and the page says nothing on washing or fading.[^3] No repair service is documented.

## Today
About 200 tsuriamiki still run, all in Wakayama, which the maker calls the only place in the world they run.[^1] Heddels says a few loopwheel machines still run in small factories in Japan, Canada and Germany, a conflict with the maker's "only place" wording that is kept as stated.[^2] Heddels names Studio D'Artisan, Strike Gold, Merz b. Schwanen and Momotaro as other specialists.[^2]

## Design
Plain crew and hooded sweatshirts where the knit, the missing side seams and the flat seams are the details. Scored on taste: slow-machine, function-first, Japanese craft, with no decoration.

## Depth
Machine history, factory names, speeds, the seam-free tube construction and the comparison with sinker knits are documented, and a Heddels explainer places it in the "selvedge of knitwear" frame.[^1][^2] The vault's workwear-and-sportswear research uses it as a case.
