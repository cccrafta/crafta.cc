## In One Line
Japanese reproduction maker of US military garments; the vault's reference case for how exactly a current maker rebuilds an issued garment.

## Lineage
The parent company, Toyo Enterprises, began as Kosho & Co. in the 1940s making souvenir garments for US servicemen in Japan; the Buzz Rickson's brand dates from 1993 [^1]. The name comes from Steve McQueen's character in The War Lover (1961) [^1]. The lineage is therefore a Japanese souvenir trade turned reproduction house, not an original US contractor.

## Making and Material
Heddels describes reproduction by infrared spectroscopy for thread colour, restored vintage machinery and molecular-level material analysis, all as the maker's account [^1]. A retailer page for the CPO shirt (BR24961) reports 100% melton wool, boiled and then sun-dried, Union Special machines, urea fouled-anchor buttons and a first-model single scalloped breast pocket; a counter-rotated right cuff appears only on that page [^2]. The tropical combat jacket BR12247 follows the first-pattern 1963-64 model in 5.5oz cotton, made in Japan [^3].

## Patina and Care
No source in the vault describes how the garments age. The melton CPO is a dense, boiled wool, a point the vault's CPO research develops from other sources.

## Today
US sales began in 2003 after a William Gibson novel, Pattern Recognition, prompted an all-black MA-1 line [^1]. Permanent Style groups the label with Clutch Cafe's "faithful recreations" [^4]. Peanuts patch editions show the label also plays with its own product [^3].

## Design
The design is the original issue pattern; changes are in finish, not cut. Design score reflects the choice to copy the right model (first-model CPO, first-pattern tropical coat) and to reproduce the details.

## Depth
Three Heddels pieces and a retailer page give enough to study how one maker handles three garment families: flight jacket, CPO shirt and tropical combat coat. The CPO shirt research in the vault uses these notes as its main contemporary-maker evidence.
