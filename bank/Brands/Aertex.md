## In One Line
A cellular cotton cloth that the British Army used in jungle-green for Far East clothing in 1943-44. It matters to Crafta as the British counterpart to American jungle fatigue development.

## Lineage
Cellular cloth with air held in its weave, patented in the late 1880s [unsourced]. The documented use in the vault is military: after fighting in Singapore, Malaya and Burma in khaki drill showed the cloth was wrong in colour and material for jungle, jungle-green Aertex clothing followed [^1].

## Making and Material
A battledress-style Aertex blouse was in widespread use from early 1944; it rode up and chafed at the waist, so a four-pocket, epauletted Aertex bush jacket was adopted as the more acceptable fix [^1]. The cloth is described as cellular, with open cells that hold air [unsourced]. No weights, mills or factory locations are recorded in the vault.

No source.

## Today
Aertex still sells cellular cotton shirting under its name [unsourced]. The vault has no current-maker coverage.

## Design
The 1940s bush jacket shows the design logic: four pockets, epaulettes, a cut that stays down at the waist [^1]. The modern line is plain shirting [unsourced].

## Depth
One IWM object record: a bush jacket worn by Lance Corporal Perry, 1st Bn Bedfordshire & Hertfordshire Regiment, on Operation Thursday, the Chindit operation of 1944 [^1]. Worth a research note on cellular cloth, its original patent and the khaki-drill failure.
