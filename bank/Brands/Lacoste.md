## In One Line
The tennis-player brand that put a visible logo on a knit polo; its date is contested between a 1926-27 tennis shirt and a 1933 company.

## Lineage
Lacoste's page says the L.12.12 was "invented by René Lacoste himself in 1933", in small honeycombed petit piqué cotton [^1]. The name decodes as 1 petit piqué, 2 short sleeves, 12 prototypes [^1]. Heddels says Lacoste won the US Open in the short-sleeve shirt in 1926-27, and that in 1933 Lacoste and André Gillier founded La Chemise Lacoste, widely regarded as the first brand with visible external branding [^2]. These dates conflict (1926-27 against 1933) and are kept as found; the 1933 date and the "free movement" intent are single-source maker claims [^1]. Heddels adds that Lacoste was not first to sell knitted polos, John Smedley was [^2]. Taylor Stitch, a low-reliability retailer blog, dates the short-sleeve knit to the 1920s [^3]. The Oxford Student says Lacoste and Fred Perry were founded by sportsmen [^4].

## Making and Material
The vault names the cloth, petit piqué, and says polyester later replaced piqué for fade resistance [^1][^2]. It has no source on current factories or construction.

## Patina and Care
No source in the vault.

## Today
Heddels records the 1951 Izod licence, adoption by 1960s Ivy Leaguers and Ralph Lauren's 1972 polo [^2]. Racket patents date from 1960 and 1963, and the "crocodile" nickname from a Boston press piece in 1923 [^1].

## Design
A knit polo with a flat collar, a short placket and a small crocodile on the chest. The logo placement is the signature, and the piqué gives the shirt its surface.

## Depth
The origin dispute, the Izod licence and the Ralph Lauren polo make a sound research thread on how a sport shirt becomes a status shirt. Evidence is mostly the brand's own and Heddels.
