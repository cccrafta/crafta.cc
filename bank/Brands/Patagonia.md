## In One Line
Climbing-hardware maker that turned a 1970 Scottish rugby shirt into a clothing line, and now sells both workwear-spec canvas pants and climbing pants with a repair guarantee; the reason it is the vault's best example of workwear and sport sharing one lineage.

## Lineage
In 1970 Yvon Chouinard bought a regulation team rugby shirt in Scotland to climb in; its collar kept gear slings off his neck.[^1][^2] Heddels says the shirt was made by Umbro and was yellow and blue striped; Patagonia's own page describes blue with one yellow and two red stripes, so the colour is in conflict.[^1][^2] By 1970 Chouinard Equipment was the largest US climbing hardware supplier; pitons that damaged rock gave way to aluminium chocks in the 1972 catalogue.[^1][^2] The clothing line took the Patagonia name and Fitzroy logo in 1972 per Heddels, 1973 per other sources.[^1] Chouinard's father was a tradesman who repaired looms at a woollen mill; Chouinard taught himself blacksmithing in 1957.[^1]

## Making and Material
The Stand Up shorts were sewn from number 10 canvas duck, and the name came from a foreman's wife sewing them on a leather-patch walking-foot machine.[^1] The Iron Forge Hemp pants use a 12.9-oz canvas, 55% industrial hemp, 27% recycled polyester and 18% organic cotton, sewn in a Fair Trade Certified factory, with double knees, boot-fit legs and a back cinch strap.[^3] The Hampi climbing pants are 53% organic hemp, 44% recycled polyester, 3% elastane, with a gusset, articulated knees and a waistband that lies flat under a harness.[^4] A Hong Kong rugby-shirt order of 3,000 a month arrived late and poor and nearly bankrupted the company.[^1] No factory is named.

## Patina and Care
Patagonia's Ironclad Guarantee: "damage from wear and tear is repaired for a reasonable charge", and used pairs resell on Worn Wear at roughly USD 32 to 65 against USD 79 to 95 new.[^3] One customer review says knees wear through after about two years and are sent back for repair; another says a pair is "holding tough" after 10 years; both are anecdotal.[^3] Design philosophy sources say a weakest component sets a product's life, so repairability and fewer parts matter.[^6]

## Today
Patagonia sells the workwear-spec Iron Forge as a work line separate from its climbing line.[^3] Its Capilene came from a 1984 Chicago trade-show look at Milliken's polyester football jerseys, and Synchilla (fall 1985) from Malden Mills; Heddels places both in the late 1970s, which the company's own history contradicts.[^2][^1] Former Chouinard contractor Mike Graham founded Gramicci from the same gear shop.[^8]

## Design
Chouinard cites Dieter Rams, "good design is as little design as possible", for functionally driven design; Vitsœ's ten principles include "long-lasting" and "honest".[^6][^7] The Hampi pants show construction shaped by the movement of climbing. Design is a taste score: function-led and in the owner's body-under-load interest, but the line is also broad and colour-led.

## Depth
Primary company pages, a Gear Patrol interview with historian Rachel Gross and Patagonia's archivist on 1970s climbing kit, and cross links to Gramicci, rugby and Rams.[^5] The weak spots are factory-level making and independent testing of the guarantee.
