## In One Line
American canvas sneaker maker whose 1917 All Star is a basketball shoe built on vulcanised rubber and canvas. A good case of a sport shoe turned everyday.

## Lineage
Converse was founded in 1908; the All Star launched in 1917 for basketball with a rubber sole and a high-top cotton-based canvas upper, and was slow to catch on [^1]. Chuck Taylor joined in 1921; the signature patch dates "by 1923", a date other sources dispute [^1]. A 1917 Spalding catalogue shows canvas rubber-soled shoes on sale in the year the All Star launched [^2]. Marugo's chairman says "Converse started making rubber-soled sneakers in 1917", framing rubber soles as a near-simultaneous invention [^3].

## Making and Material
The All Star's construction is the vulcanised rubber sole on a canvas upper; Research/Canvas notes vulcanisation at about 170 degrees C needs canvas. Present-day factories and mill sources are `[unsourced]`.

## Patina and Care
Canvas and rubber scuff and soften with wear `[unsourced]`; no care or repair offer found.

## Today
Worn by the first US Olympic basketball team in 1936 and nearly every NBA player in 1949; last worn in an NBA game by Tree Rollins in 1979 [^1]. A Converse co-branded high-top sold 30,000 pairs in 2011 for Dashing Tweeds [^5]; Nigel Cabourn collaborations included Converse [^4]. Nepenthes LA lists Converse among its collaborations.

## Design
The All Star high-top is a durable shape: toe cap, canvas upper, simple sole. Collaboration churn is a different matter.

## Depth
The patch date dispute, the 1917 simultaneity with Spalding and Marugo, and the canvas-to-vulcanisation link make a research topic of its own.
