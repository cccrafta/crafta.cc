## In One Line
Dense, untreated long-staple cotton that sheds rain because its fibres swell when wet. A material, not a clothing label; the origin story is the maker's, the patent is not found.

## Lineage
The maker dates Ventile to 1943 and names the Shirley Institute as its source, with the RAF as the intended user; both are maker claims, and the patent was not found.[^1][^2] Grenfell Cloth, made by Haythornthwaite in Burnley from 1922-23, is a related dense Egyptian cotton, used on Everest in 1933 and 1936.[^1] Retail claims of a tenfold survival gain, 80% pilot survival and Everest 1953 are unverified marketing.[^5]

## Making and Material
Ventile uses extra-long-staple cotton woven to very high density, with no membrane or coating; the fibres swell when wet and close the gaps.[^1][^2] US 4,286,012 quotes an Ashton Brothers description: fine long-staple cotton, doubled yarn, Oxford weave, and a prior all-cotton Oxford fabric in Pima at 72/2 by 60/2 yarns, 184 by 80 per inch greige, 196 by 82 finished.[^3] That is a 1970s trade description, not the 1940s spec. Wikipedia (T4) adds weights of 145-300 g/m2 and trademark ownership by Stotz & Co AG of Switzerland since 2017, and is not used for scoring.

## Patina and Care
No source in the vault on how Ventile ages or re-proofs.

## Today
Private White V.C. makes a Ventile Harrington in Manchester.[^4] Albam's Alpine jacket uses it, at about £20 per metre against £1-2 for ordinary cotton.[^5] PWVC x Raeburn made a reversible piece.[^6] Nigel Cabourn uses it often, and Fujito made a Ventile jungle jacket for Trunk.[^7][^8]

## Design
The cloth sets the garment: dense, quiet and crisp, with the water-shedding work done by the weave and fibre rather than by a coating. Judged from the maker and retailer descriptions.[^1][^5]
