## Overview
"Innovations in textile" is a very wide field, so this note filters for Crafta's interests: fabric as engineering, workwear and performance, Japanese makers, and repair/circularity. Reading across the sources, four threads matter: waterproofing without fluorine, recycling that is real but tiny, fermented protein fibre that is in production, and mills blending regenerated cellulose into traditional cloth. Where a maker or fair organiser is the source (T1) the claim is the maker's; trade and vendor material is T3, and market figures from research-firm blurbs are treated as indicative. Related: [[Research/Workwear and Sportswear - Function and Ontology]], [[Research/Canvas]].

[inference] The oldest waterproof cloths (waxed cotton, tight-woven cotton) never needed fluorochemicals, which is why the PFAS shift lands hardest on membranes and DWR coatings and not on those cloths. No source read says this; verify before using.

## Key Details

### 1. Waterproofing without PFAS
- Gore-Tex has moved from an ePTFE to an ePE (expanded polyethylene) membrane. Gore's own page calls the laminate "PFAS-free* with a reduced carbon footprint**"; the footnote reads "made without intentionally added per- and polyfluorinated substances; may contain trace amounts", and the claim includes the DWR [^4].
- Gore's page gives no thickness, pore, hydrostatic-head or breathability figures, only "durably waterproof", "very breathable" and "totally windproof" [^4]. Trek & Mountain reports Gore's minimum hydrostatic head as 30,000mm and Gore's claim of equal durability, and that Gore offered "no figures or commitments" on breathability [^5].
- Independent evidence is field impressions, not lab data. Trek & Mountain, after several months in a Mountain Equipment Makalu jacket, found it reliably dry and fine for steady walking but "clearly not quite as breathable as GORE-TEX PRO" [^5]. Live For The Outdoors, testing Patagonia and Mountain Equipment ePE jackets, noticed no difference apart from a thinner feel [^6]. No published side-by-side lab breathability test of ePE against ePTFE turned up in searches.
- The weak point both testers describe is the finish, not the membrane: the PFC-free DWR "simply stopped beading water" on the cuffs and other handled areas, which Trek & Mountain calls standard for PFC-free finishes [^5].
- "No intentionally added PFAS" is narrower than "no fluorine"; Live For The Outdoors notes Gore removed the PFCs "of environmental concern", agreed with Greenpeace, not every fluorinated compound [^6].
- Techtextil 2026 honoured two PFAS-free water-repellent finishes: EC0Tex (Bäumlin & Ernst with Empa; dry plasma coats each filament with an organosilicon layer; products planned within 12 months) and H&B Materials (plant fatty acids grafted onto cellulose; the 5/5 spray-test scores are the winner's claim) [^20].
- Regulation: France's February 2025 law bans PFAS-containing clothing from 2026 and all textiles from 2030, with exemptions to be set by later decrees [^23]; the Techtextil organiser's release independently says the sale of PFAS-containing apparel is being phased out from January 2026 [^20]. The earlier forecast that most jackets would be PFAS-free by default by 2026/27 was single-sourced and has been dropped.

### 2. Recycling: real, tiny
- Less than 1% of global fibre is from pre- and post-consumer recycled textiles (Textile Exchange, 2024 data); most recycled fibre is polyester from plastic bottles, 6.9% of all fibre [^11]. Textiles Intelligence (via Textile Focus) adds that about 11% of post-consumer textile waste is collected and sorted for recycling, recycled polyester costs about 2.6 times virgin, and Europe reaching 15% textile-to-textile recycling by 2035 would need EUR 8–11 bn capex; those three figures rest on one report and have not been checked against a second source [^1].
- Why Renewcell failed (bankruptcy February 2024), per Trellis: its pulp cost nearly twice wood pulp and fibre makers' viscose from it was priced 50% above regular viscose; brands signed up for capsule collections but few moved to store lines; pulp deliveries fell to zero in November 2023 after 2,800 tonnes shipped in July; and H&M ended its support. It also had to build a collector, sorter, fibre-maker and mill chain from scratch, and sold pulp rather than cloth [^7].
- What Circulose became: Altor bought the assets in June 2024; the company dropped open-market sales for a partner-led, licensing-based model, and its Sundsvall plant is scheduled to restart in Q4 2026 with a stated 60,000 tonnes a year [^9] [^8]. Nothing read confirms the restart has happened.
- Circulose brand partners announced 24 June 2026: Eileen Fisher, Marimekko, Samsøe Samsøe and King Louie, joining H&M Group, Mango, BESTSELLER, Reformation and Marks & Spencer; the release gives no volumes [^8]. By its February 2026 announcement 11 brands had committed [^9].
- Circulose and Spinnova: the partnership aims at a mechanical, cotton-like fibre; no capacity or timeline is given [^2].
- Circ (Danville, Virginia; a different company from Circulose) separates polyester and cellulose from polycotton. Its Fiber Club added Madewell, Reformation and C&A in February 2026; each brand develops collections in TENCEL™ | Circ® lyocell with 30% Circ pulp from recycled polycotton, and Circ's first industrial-scale plant is described as "underway in France" [^10].

### 3. Fermented protein fibre
- Spiber's Brewed Protein fibre is in commercial products: 17 products from four labels at the 29 September 2023 launch [^12], five Goldwin brands in the original March 2023 announcement [^13], and 45 brands and 193 products by August 2025 per Spiber [^15].
- Polymer is made at a plant in Rayong, Thailand; Spiber's 2019 announcement gave "several hundred tonnes per year" capacity [^14]. A 500 tonnes-a-year figure appears in secondary sources only and was not verified; the sources read do not say where the fibre is spun.
- Goldwin's stated goal is for 10% of newly developed products to use Brewed Protein by 2030 [^13]; no source read reports progress against it.
- The 2023 Nuptse Jacket is 60% Brewed Protein, 25% wool and 15% nylon on the outer, with a PTFE waterproof/breathable laminate on the reverse layer: the fermented fibre sits on the outside of a fluoropolymer membrane [^13].
- Mobility: a Land Cruiser Prado concept with Brewed Protein seat covers was shown in 2023; the production NEWSCAPE, released spring 2026 (a 7 March 2026 start date appears in press coverage that was not read), is what Spiber calls the first commercial mobility use [^16].
- Spiber says end-of-life recovery into feedstock has been shown at lab scale only [^15]. No source read describes how the fibre wears or ages, so patina evidence is absent.
- Market-report claims about spider-silk textiles are unreliable; the fibre is real but small.

### 4. Mills blending regenerated cellulose
- SAISEI (October 2024): Kaihara Denim, Lenzing and Roica by Asahi Kasei; Lenzing ECOVERO with REFIBRA (up to 20% post-consumer textile waste per the release body, a minimum of 20% recycled content per its footnote) and ROICA EF recycled stretch yarn; three fabrications including a low-stretch selvedge [^17] [^18].
- REMOD (14-15 October 2026): Lenzing's collection woven at Kaihara's integrated mill in Hiroshima in three TENCEL™ lyocell types (Standard, HV100, dtex 2.2), including heavyweight jackets and cotton-free shirting. HV100's Variocut fibre-length variation is meant to echo the unevenness of cotton; these are vendor claims with no fibre ratios or wear data. Lenzing says TENCEL has been in Kaihara's collections since the 1990s [^19].
- Kaihara is widely reported to make about half of Japanese denim (trade and academic sources seen only as search summaries; neither the SAISEI release nor the Just Style article says it), so treat the figure as indicative.
- Tension to explore: a selvedge purist's cloth versus a stretch and lyocell blend.

### 5. Bio-based synthetics and mass balance
- Under ISCC PLUS, mass balance means certified and non-certified material "can be physically mixed during storage, transport or processing, but are kept segregated on a bookkeeping basis" [^21]. [inference] A biomass-balanced yarn therefore need not physically contain bio-based material; the claim is an accounting one. The guidance document is marked v1.0, under public consultation and "not valid yet", so this is the scheme's stated concept, not final rules.
- Asahi Kasei's ROICA page lists ISCC PLUS mass-balance certification among its plant certifications; it shows no certificate number, and the ISCC certificate database was not queried, so which site holds it is unconfirmed [^22].
- The biomass-balanced nylon 6.6 concept shown with Asahi Kasei is from NILIT, an Israeli maker and out of scope under AGENTS.md, so it is not developed here; the FashionUnited outlook that mentions it is used for general direction only [^3].
