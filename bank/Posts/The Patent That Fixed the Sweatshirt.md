## Content

<p>Two sweatshirts at Champion Heritage Times Harajuku sell for JPY 20,000 plus tax each, and what separates them is a seam and a gusset. The reissue of the 1938 shirt has seamless one-piece shoulders and a V-shaped gusset at the neck; the store presents the 1952 shirt as the first with vertical gussets down its sides, which it calls the expansion gusset. Both constructions were patented because a cotton sweatshirt kept failing in the wash, and the order in which they arrived records what broke first.</p>

<p>Before cotton, athletic jerseys were wool shaped on the machine: Wright &amp; Ditson's 1901 catalogue sold football jerseys "knit to shape of body and arms", and Spalding in 1917 described its jerseys as "fashioned or knit to exact shape on the machine and then put together by hand". Russell Athletic says Benny Russell, then playing football at Alabama, proposed an all-cotton practice jersey in 1926 to replace "the itchy, chafing wool uniforms" and began producing the crew-neck sweatshirt in 1930; Heddels credits Benjamin Russell Jr. with the blueprint in 1920. Champion, founded in Rochester in 1919 by the Feinbloom family, built its trade on University of Michigan teams, and Heddels names what those teams handed back: laundering shrank and warped the kit.</p>

<p>Samuel Friedland's patent, US 2,126,186, filed 20 January 1938 and granted 9 August, describes the ordinary shirt as at least four pieces knitted with the wales, or ribs, running lengthwise. Washed, it "necessarily becomes much shorter" and the waistband shrinks up, and pulling it back down strains the fibres and the seams until, too often, the seams split. Friedland cut his blanks across a knitted tube so the wales lie horizontally through the body and across the shoulders, with front, back and sleeves in one piece and no seam where sleeve meets body. With the rib turned sideways, the body can be stretched lengthwise after any number of washes, so a wearer pulls the lost length back out, and the seams that remain run down the sides and under the sleeves, where the patent says they meet less strain. Gussets give stretch across the shoulders and at the neck for players wearing bulky pads. Friedland also counted it an advantage that a body stretched lengthwise narrows in circumference, "conforming more snugly" to the wearer.</p>

<p>US 2,613,360, filed by Friedland and William Feinbloom on 13 August 1951 and granted on 14 October 1952, takes up that narrowing: it builds on the 1938 shirt and reports that after repeated laundering "there frequently occurs greater shrinkage in width" than can be overcome, describing a body "subject to reduction in width when laundered or stretched longitudinally". The pull that restored length was taking it out of the girth. Vertical expansion gussets at the sides, knitted with their wales at right angles to the body's, give across the width where the body gives along the length; sweat pants received the same gusset on the inner leg, leaving the outer leg unbroken. The close fit stayed, because the patent warns that loose fabric may become "entangled" with another player's arms or legs and cause injury; the fit only has to leave arms, shoulders and head free.</p>

<p>Washing also marks old cotton sweatshirts through colour. Samual Ward of Vertical Archives, quoted by Wear/Ware, says vintage grey and white sweatshirts tend to hold their colour while red and blue fade through wash cycles and sun, red moving toward salmon or pink "due to the all cotton fabric". He likens it to the whiskers on old Levi's and says dealers rate faded pieces higher because only years of wear produce them.</p>

<p>Heddels reported in 2018 that most Reverse Weave was blended with some polyester and made in Cambodia or Honduras, with an occasional Made in USA line sold largely in Japan. The Harajuku reissues keep the positions the patents fixed: the 1938 shirt has no seam where sleeve meets body, and, by the store's account, the 1952 shirt has its gusset down the side. Each sits where a Friedland patent placed it, a seam moved and a panel added to keep a cotton shirt wearable through a team's laundry.</p>

<p>Each of those changes survives as a position on the garment, which is why it can be lifted out of it: Champion's Homage Archives line sets the expansion gusset, drawn for football sweatshirts, into a tailored jacket and trousers that will never face a team laundry. Across sport and work clothing, wear happens in particular places, so reinforcement belongs there, and movement happens at the joints, so release belongs there too. What transfers to clothing made now is the habit of asking which strain each panel answers; a gusset copied without that question keeps the shape and loses its reason.</p>
