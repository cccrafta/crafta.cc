## Opener

A concrete scene with a small thesis in the same paragraph. The two sweatshirts and their price anchor the reader before the claim about what separates them; the last sentence states what the post will show.

<p>Two sweatshirts at Champion Heritage Times Harajuku sell for JPY 20,000 plus tax each, and what separates them is a seam and a gusset. The reissue of the 1938 shirt has seamless one-piece shoulders and a V-shaped gusset at the neck; the store presents the 1952 shirt as the first with vertical gussets down its sides, which it calls the expansion gusset. Both constructions were patented because a cotton sweatshirt kept failing in the wash, and the order in which they arrived records what broke first.</p>

## Ending

Two paragraphs. The first says where the subject stands now and names what each seam answers; the second reads the garment as a record of problems solved, cites a real maker moving the detail into another garment, and says what transfers to clothing made today. It returns to the seam and gusset the opener put on the table.

<p>Heddels reported in 2018 that most Reverse Weave was blended with some polyester and made in Cambodia or Honduras, with an occasional Made in USA line sold largely in Japan. The Harajuku reissues keep the positions the patents fixed: the 1938 shirt has no seam where sleeve meets body, and, by the store's account, the 1952 shirt has its gusset down the side. Each sits where a Friedland patent placed it, a seam moved and a panel added to keep a cotton shirt wearable through a team's laundry.</p>

<p>Each of those changes survives as a position on the garment, which is why it can be lifted out of it: Champion's Homage Archives line sets the expansion gusset, drawn for football sweatshirts, into a tailored jacket and trousers that will never face a team laundry. Across sport and work clothing, wear happens in particular places, so reinforcement belongs there, and movement happens at the joints, so release belongs there too. What transfers to clothing made now is the habit of asking which strain each panel answers; a gusset copied without that question keeps the shape and loses its reason.</p>
