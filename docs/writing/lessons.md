# Writing Lessons

Running log of corrections that shaped Crafta's prose. The rules live in [[editorial-framework]]; this page holds the lessons behind them, the patterns that keep recurring, and the drafting process. If this page and the framework disagree, the framework wins. Add a lesson whenever the owner corrects a draft, with the date and the post.

## Drafting process

1. Read `editorial-framework.md` before drafting, not only before the audit.
2. Plan the opener and the ending together (see Openers and endings).
3. Draft, then run the humanize skill exhaustively: every numbered pattern, each checked against the full text, findings reported even when a pattern has no matches. A first pass once missed a negative parallelism (pattern 9) that the owner then caught; spot-checking breaks trust in the whole workflow.
4. Audit against the framework and the `generate-posts` checklist, then present to the owner.

## Openers and endings

- **Opener:** a concrete scene or failure that hooks from the first sentence, followed by a small thesis in the same paragraph (what the post will show). A paragraph that only announces what the post will do ("This piece follows…") is a table of contents, not a hook. Source: "The Patent That Fixed the Sweatshirt", 2026-10-11.
- **Ending:** one or two bigger-picture paragraphs. First where the subject stands now, then what the design reveals (its ontology: a record of problems solved) and what transfers to clothing made today. The ending resolves what the opener set up, so opener, body and ending read as one piece. Reference: the last two paragraphs of that post.
- **No recap endings.** Do not close by retelling the sequence the body just told.

## Patterns that keep recurring

- **Negative parallelism.** Never "No X, no Y. Just Z." State the positive fact: "The fabric is plain cotton; the cut does the work." Use a clause ("not waxed or coated") if the contrast matters.
- **Paragraph buttons.** Do not end paragraphs with a short mic-drop sentence under about 10 words ("It is how you identify the original."). Fold it into the previous sentence or cut it. Not every paragraph needs a closer.
- **Punchy reveals.** Do not set up a fact and land it as its own short sentence. Fold it into the description with a conjunction or clause.
- **Announcing openers.** Do not open a paragraph with a sentence describing what the paragraph will do. Start one sentence later, on the concrete subject.
- **Stacked short sentences** used for rhythm. Combine them into one honest sentence.
- **Abstract stances.** A closing that could apply to any well-designed object is too abstract. Name the makers, the mechanism, the context.

## Observation and sourcing

- **Do not invent sensory detail.** If no source says how a garment feels or looks, the draft stays with the sourced cause and effect (what a patent describes happening) and says plainly that the observation is thin. Ask the owner for first-hand observation instead of filling the gap.
- **Patina is required** for materials and garments posts. When only one low-reliability source exists, attribute it, scope it to what it covers (vintage dyed cotton in general, not one brand), and flag it as thin in the post's Notes.
- **Verify a thesis against the primary text before building the post on it.** A causal link between two documents ("the second patent fixes the cost of the first") is an interpretation until the text supports it; check the patents or catalogues, and soften the claim to what they state.
- **Patent and archive OCR is garbled.** Paraphrase instead of quoting truncated fragments.
- **Fetch summaries are second-hand.** Note which claims rest on a summary, and do not present them as verified.

## Before and after

Pairs taken from the owner's corrections, kept so the pattern is recognisable next time.

- **Declarative profundity.** Bad: "The warmth-to-weight ratio IS the design." Good: "A wool sweater thick enough to insulate under a jacket adds bulk to the shoulder. A cashmere sweater does not." Show the property working in a specific garment; the reader reaches the conclusion without being told.
- **Declaring instead of observing.** Bad: "cashmere is the material whose physics determine the garment." Good: "The fibre is too fine to hold structure, which is why cashmere follows the body rather than imposing a shape." Another good case: "Bond wears an N.Peal cashmere sweater under a Barbour jacket in the Scotland scenes; a wool sweater of the same warmth would crowd the shoulder."
- **Measurements as decoration.** "14 to 19 microns" means nothing to most readers. Use a number when it aids a comparison, and give it physical context ("finer than a human hair"). Never repeat one measurement as a refrain.
- **Negative parallelism.** Bad: "No waxed cotton, no rubber lining. Just the geometry of the cut directing water off the back." Good: "The fabric is plain cotton; the geometry of the cut does the work."
- **Paragraph buttons.** Bad closers: "It is how you identify the original." "That is where beauty lives." "The rest is history." Fold the idea into the previous sentence or cut it. Varying which paragraphs get a closer matters; buttoning every paragraph is itself the pattern.
- **Punchy reveal.** Bad: "Pick it up and the weight is wrong." Good: "Pick up a CPO shirt and the weight doesn't match the silhouette: placket, collar, two chest pockets and a straight hem all read as shirting, but the fabric is dense melton, the same compressed wool a peacoat is built from."
- **Announcing opener.** Bad: "Brands that treat the CPO as a starting point rather than a specification show their hand in what they change first." Good: "Engineered Garments has been revisiting the CPO silhouette for nearly two decades: snap placket instead of buttons…" The theme emerges from the examples and does not need stating first.
- **Abstract stance.** Bad: "when every element answers a specific need, the design does not age", and dramatic restatements such as "rain still needs shedding, arms still need to swing". The stance that worked names who, what and the recognition: coat-makers built the Harrington to handle rain on a golf course, and that practical specificity, rather than any design ambition, gave the jacket a life well beyond the course. Name the distinction itself, never "that distinction".
- **Let the story carry it.** "Cucinelli bought 20kg of cashmere from a yarn merchant he couldn't pay, made 60 sweaters, and drove to Germany because Germans paid on time" interests the reader more than any statement about what cashmere is worth.

## Style references

- **Reference passages:** [[examples]] holds the sweatshirt opener and ending as copyable models.
- **Heddels:** plain, specific, fact-building.
- **Monocle:** journalistic directness, interested without being breathless.
- **Permanent Style:** honest assessment, direct and useful.
